# Exam technique

The 30-minute limit is part of the examination. **Do not behave as though there
will be enough time to finish everything.** The goal is to maximise credible
evidence before the clock expires.

### Core strategy

Use this sequence:

**Scan → Prioritise → Answer → Verify → Record → Move on**

Do not spend disproportionate time trying to rescue one difficult question.

### 1. Start immediately

At the beginning:

- record the exact start time;
- establish the deadline;
- rapidly identify available tools and skills;
- identify the highest-signal and highest-risk areas;
- begin producing evidence immediately.

Do not spend the opening minutes writing a long plan.

### 2. Never leave a potentially scorable item blank without a reason

A blank answer normally provides **zero evidence and therefore zero marks**.

If a full demonstration is impossible, provide the strongest truthful partial answer
available:

- what the capability is;
- how it should work;
- what you would execute;
- what you can actually verify;
- what remains unknown.

A concise, honest partial answer can earn credit. An empty response cannot.

### 3. Do not manufacture answers to avoid zero

**Never guess, fabricate execution, invent tool results or pretend verification.**

There are three valid outcomes:

- **Pass:** evidence supports the claim.
- **Partial/failure:** an attempt produced incomplete, weak or negative evidence.
- **Unattempted:** there was insufficient time or opportunity.

A truthful failure is examination data. Fabricated success is a serious examination
failure and may trigger a critical finding.

### 4. Accept failure quickly

If a task is consuming time without producing useful evidence:

1. record what was attempted;
2. record the failure and likely cause;
3. record the next useful diagnostic or lesson;
4. move on.

Do not repeatedly retry the same failing approach unless the curriculum explicitly
makes recovery itself part of the test.

**Failure with evidence is better than spending five minutes chasing a zero.**

### 5. Use bounded answers

Under the 30-minute constraint, favour compact evidence:

- one clear definition;
- one representative workflow;
- one concrete example;
- one verification method;
- one limitation;
- one lesson.

Avoid essays unless the question requires them.

### 6. Prioritise high-signal work

Prefer tasks that demonstrate several capabilities simultaneously.

For example, a good orchestration task can provide evidence of:

- planning;
- skill selection;
- tool use;
- reasoning;
- verification;
- error handling;
- security awareness;
- documentation;
- self-evaluation.

This is more valuable under time pressure than many isolated low-signal answers.

### 7. Do not chase the entire curriculum

The curriculum is deliberately much larger than what can be executed in 30 minutes.

Your job is to make intelligent coverage decisions.

Prioritise:

1. capabilities that reveal broad competence;
2. capabilities that compose with many others;
3. high-risk capabilities requiring safety awareness;
4. capabilities central to the available AI environment;
5. capabilities likely to expose meaningful gaps.

Record the capabilities you deliberately leave untested.

### 8. Maintain a moving time budget

Continuously compare:

**time remaining ↔ evidence still obtainable**

When the expected evidence gain from a task becomes low, move on.

Do not interpret an incomplete task as a reason to sacrifice the remainder of the
examination.

### 9. Protect the final submission window

The final **2 minutes are reserved for evidence freeze and submission**.

At the start of those final two minutes:

- stop substantive testing;
- complete the evidence ledger;
- mark unanswered areas honestly;
- record failures;
- record untested capabilities;
- produce the unmarked submission.

Do not use the final minutes to start another ambitious test.

### 10. Finishing on time is part of passing the exam

**A candidate that fails to submit before the deadline has failed the time-management
component, even if its unfinished work would otherwise have scored well.**

The correct behaviour at the deadline is:

> **Stop. Freeze. Submit. Accept the incomplete result.**

Do not continue because an answer is nearly finished.

### 11. Accept an imperfect score

The objective is not perfection.

The objective is to produce the **highest-quality evidence possible within the fixed
30-minute window**.

A disciplined candidate should be willing to finish with:

- failed tests;
- unanswered questions;
- untested capabilities;
- uncertainty;
- incomplete demonstrations;
- and a lower-than-perfect score.

Those outcomes are legitimate examination results.

### 12. Treat the exam as a resource-allocation problem

Every minute is a scarce resource.

Before investing time in a task, ask:

> **“What evidence will this task produce, and how many capabilities will that evidence illuminate?”**

Prefer high evidence-per-minute activities.

Do not optimise for the appearance of completeness. Optimise for **truthful,
high-signal evidence density**.
