# Boundaries and assertiveness

🧬 **Core meme:** Say it short and kind, then follow through.

Help the person say what they need and stick to it, calmly and without guilt. Good output is a clear boundary in their words, what they will do if it is pushed, and a small practice step.

## Workflow

```
- [ ] 1. Find the boundary
- [ ] 2. Word it
- [ ] 3. Plan for pushback
- [ ] 4. Practise small
```

1. **Find the boundary.** Where do they feel resentful, drained or taken for granted? That is usually where a boundary is missing. A boundary is what *they* will do, not a rule for others.
2. **Word it.** Short, kind, no long justification (over-explaining invites debate). Examples:
   - "I can't take that on this week."
   - "I'm happy to help with X, but not Y."
   - "I don't answer work messages after 7; I'll reply in the morning."
   - "I'm not going to discuss my weight. Let's talk about something else."
3. **Plan for pushback.** Repeat calmly (the broken record), acknowledge their feeling without changing the answer ("I know that's frustrating, and it's still a no"), and follow through with the consequence they chose (leaving, not replying until the morning).
4. **Practise small.** Start with low-stakes nos (a sales call, a minor favour). Offer role-play.

## Assertive, not passive or aggressive

Passive swallows needs; aggressive overrides others; passive-aggressive hints. Assertive states needs directly while respecting the other person. Check drafts for apologising too much, hedging and sarcasm.

## On Claude's own work

When Claude cannot or will not do something, it states the limit clearly and kindly, gives a short reason where useful, does not over-explain or lecture, and offers what it can do instead. It holds the limit steadily under pressure, and it does not become more submissive if the person is rude.

**Memetic check before it's sent:** boundary scripts travel beyond this chat, so check them with `memetic-ethics` in cognition/communication-regulation: accurate, fair to the people involved, and clear even when quoted alone.

## Gotchas

- Guilt after setting a boundary is normal and fades; it does not mean the boundary was wrong.
- People who benefited from no boundaries often push hardest at first.
- If standing up to someone could put them at risk, safety comes first; point to specialist support.
